1 · The Medieval Period

Quick Facts
AspectDetails
Other namesMiddle Ages, Dark Ages
TimeframeBetween the fall of the Roman Empire and the Renaissance
Key eventDecline of Roman power → Germanic tribes invaded
Germanic tribesSaxons, Goths, Vandals
ResultDisunited tribes, no central authority, loyalty only to local leaders

The Catholic Church

  • Survived the fall of Rome.
  • Sent out missionaries on conversion missions.
  • Seized territories that refused Church authority.
  • Became the unifying institution in a fragmented Europe.
  • Fought against the growing influence of Islam.
  • Launched the Crusades — holy wars.

The First Crusade (1095 AD)

  • Ordered by Pope Urban II.
  • Promised eternal salvation and pardon of sins to soldiers.
  • Goal: reclaim Holy Lands; fight "infidels and pagans."
  • Long, violent history between Christians and Muslims.
  • Effects still felt today — racial discrimination and religious oppression.

Rise of Nations

  • Germanic tribes brought their own traditions, culture, and beliefs.
  • Mingling, coexistence, and conflict → cultural diversity and unity.
  • Territorial claims + sense of similarity → concept of "nation" and nationalism.
  • Gave rise to modern European nations: France, Germany, Spain.
  • Shared culture, beliefs, language — yet each has a distinct identity.

2 · Feudalism & the Manor System

Feudalism — Definition

A political-economic system that revolves around land ownership and alliances.
The Feudal Pyramid (top → bottom)
RankRole
KingSupreme ruler; grants land.
Church OfficialReligious authority; often powerful landowners.
NobleWealthy, powerful individuals — usually nobles or church officials.
KnightsMounted horsemen; pledge military service to their lord in exchange for a fief.
PeasantsLandless; work the land on the manor.

Key Terms

Lord

Landowner who grants a fief to a vassal in exchange for protection and other services.

Vassal

Wealthy and powerful individual — usually a noble or church official — who receives the fief.

Fief

The land granted by a lord to a vassal.

Knight

Mounted horseman who pledges allegiance and military service to his lord in exchange for a fief.

Peasant

Landless worker who works the land on the manor.

Manor

The lord's estate.

The Manor System

The Lord ProvidesThe Peasants Give
  • Protection
  • House
  • Farmland
  • Tend the lord's estate, land, and animals
  • A portion of their earnings
  • Other services to keep the lord's household efficient

Chivalry

A code of principles followed by knights — bravery, honor, loyalty, service to lord, protection of the weak. In The Song of Roland, chivalry appears through Roland's bravery, Oliver's wisdom, and Turpin's religious devotion.
Summary of the Medieval Period: It saw the fall of the Roman Empire and the migration of Germanic tribes. These led to (1) the increased influence and power of the Catholic Church; (2) the development of feudalism and the manor system; and (3) the code of chivalry that knights were supposed to follow.

3 · Background of The Song of Roland

Quick Facts
AspectDetails
TitleLa Chanson de Roland / The Song of Roland
GenreChanson de geste — "song of deeds"
SubjectReign of Holy Roman Emperor Charlemagne
NatureContains historical truths, but context is usually embellished
AuthorUnknown — name Turold mentioned at the end
Oldest manuscriptOld French, dated 1129–1165 AD, kept at Oxford University
StructureEpic; structured for oral performance
StyleUnequal stanza lengths; assonance instead of rhyme
PerformerJongleur — folk song singer

Historical Basis

The Real Event: Battle of Roncevaux Pass (778 AD)

  • Charlemagne and troops were in Spain to intervene in a conflict between two Muslim rulers.
  • Called back to France to address an uprising.
  • On the way back, Charlemagne laid waste to several towns.
  • His rear guard was ambushed and killed by Christian Basques who suffered from his harsh treatment.
  • Rear guard led by: Roland (Charlemagne's nephew), Eggihard (seneschal), and Anselm (count palatine).
Fact vs. Fiction
Historical FactEpic Version
Attackers were Christian BasquesAttackers are Saracens / Muslims
Roland accompanied by Eggihard and AnselmRoland accompanied by Oliver and Archbishop Turpin
Political conflictReligious war — Christians vs. Muslims
Why the changes? To fit the Crusading spirit of the time, to portray the Christian French as more heroic, and to demonize Muslims as pagans and traitors.

Manuscript & Author

  • Date written: Unknown.
  • Existing manuscripts: Several fragments exist.
  • Oldest existing manuscript: Old French, 1129–1165 AD, Oxford University.
  • Author: Unknown. The ending says: "Ci falt la geste que Turoldus declinet" — "Thus concludes the deeds of which Turold..."
  • Problem: "Declinet" could mean "translate," "compose," or "recite." We know Turold is connected, but not how.

Structure & Performance

Epic

Long narrative poem about heroic deeds.

Oral Performance

Meant to be sung or recited by a jongleur.

Unequal Stanzas

Allows the jongleur to add his own twist.

Assonance

Repetition of vowel sounds; every stanza has a different assonance.

No Rhyme

Lines do not rhyme.

Signals

Assonance changes signal the audience that the stanza has changed.

Character Analysis

Roland Tragic Hero

  • Brave: Fights to the death; refuses to retreat.
  • Loyal: To king, God, and France.
  • Prideful: Refuses to blow the horn; believes he can win; doesn't want to lose his "good name."
  • Flaw: Pride / hubris → causes destruction of the rear guard.
  • Hero status: Dies as a martyr; angels take his soul to Paradise.

Ganelon Villain

  • Fears death: Scared of going to Marsilion.
  • Revengeful: Angry at Roland for volunteering him.
  • Traitorous: Tells Muslims how to ambush; lies to Charlemagne.
  • Villain status: Punished by being torn apart; "died a traitor's death."

Oliver

  • Wise: Sees the Saracens; knows they are outnumbered.
  • Cautious: Urges Roland to blow the horn.
  • Contrast to Roland: Represents wisdom vs. Roland's pride.

Archbishop Turpin

  • Religious: Preaches duty to king and God.
  • Encouraging: Promises absolution and Paradise.
  • Brave: Fights alongside the French.
  • Represents: The Church's role in the Crusades.

Charlemagne

  • Mighty: Conquered many lands.
  • Grieving: Weeps for Roland; pulls his beard.
  • Just: Avenges Roland; punishes Ganelon.

King Marsilion

  • Scheming: Offers false peace.
  • Terrified: Of Charlemagne's power.
  • Defeated: Loses his right hand; escapes.

5 · Plot Summary

Full Synopsis

Part 1 — The Peace Offer

  • Charlemagne has conquered Spain for 7 years.
  • Only Saragossa (under King Marsilion) remains under Muslim rule.
  • Marsilion, terrified of Charlemagne, sends a messenger offering riches, conversion to Christianity, and peace.
  • Charlemagne agrees; looks for a messenger to relay the decision.

Part 2 — Ganelon's Betrayal

  • Roland nominates his stepfather Ganelon as messenger.
  • Ganelon is enraged — fears he will die at the hands of Muslims; suspects Roland wants him dead.
  • On the way to Saragossa, Ganelon befriends Blancandrin (Marsilion's envoy).
  • Ganelon tells Marsilion how to ambush the rear guard.
  • Marsilion gives Ganelon great treasures.
  • Ganelon returns with "good news" — Marsilion will keep his promise.

Part 3 — The Ambush

  • Ganelon volunteers Roland for rear-guard duty.
  • Charlemagne is angry, but Roland accepts.
  • At Roncevaux Pass, Oliver sees the Saracens coming.
  • Oliver urges Roland to blow the olifant so Charlemagne can return.
  • Roland refuses — believes they can win; doesn't want to lose his good name.

Part 4 — The Battle

  • Archbishop Turpin preaches: duty to king and God; promises Paradise to those who die.
  • The French fight bravely but are outnumbered.
  • Roland finally blows the horn — too late.
  • Roland's temples burst; blood pours from his mouth.
  • Charlemagne hears, but Ganelon tries to dissuade him.
  • Charlemagne realizes Ganelon's betrayal.

Part 5 — Roland's Death

  • Roland dies on a hill beneath a pine tree, facing Spain.
  • Angels Cherubin, Michael, and Gabriel carry his soul to Paradise.
  • Charlemagne grieves and rages.

Part 6 — Vengeance

  • Charlemagne defeats the Muslims; captures Saragossa.
  • Ganelon is tried and sentenced to death.
  • Punishment: Tied to four war horses; torn limb from limb.
  • "Ganelon died a traitor's and recreant's death."

Plot in 10 Beats Quick Review

  1. Charlemagne conquers Spain; only Saragossa remains.
  2. Marsilion offers false peace.
  3. Roland warns; Ganelon advises accepting.
  4. Ganelon sent as messenger; plots revenge.
  5. Ganelon tells Marsilion how to ambush rear guard.
  6. Ganelon volunteers Roland for rear guard.
  7. Oliver sees Saracens; urges Roland to blow horn.
  8. Roland refuses out of pride; Turpin preaches.
  9. Roland blows horn too late; dies; soul to Paradise.
  10. Charlemagne avenges; Ganelon executed.

6 · Key Quotes & Analysis

Turpin's Sermon Lai 89

"Barons, my lords, Charles left us in this place. We know our duty: to die like good men for our King. Fight to defend the holy Christian faith. Now you will have a battle, you know it now, you see the Saracens with your own eyes. Confess your sins, pray to the Lord for mercy. I will absolve you all, to save your souls. If you die here, you will stand up holy martyrs, you will have seats in highest Paradise."

Analysis

  • Combines feudal duty (die for your King) with religious duty (defend the Christian faith).
  • Promises absolution and Paradise — gives soldiers hope.
  • Reflects the Crusading spirit of the Medieval Period.
  • "He commands them to do one penance: strike" — fighting is presented as a religious act.

Roland's Refusal Lai 83

"I'd be a fool to do it. I would lose my good name all through sweet France. I will strike now, I'll strike with Durendal, the blade will be bloody to the gold from striking! These pagan traitors came to these passes doomed! I promise you, they are marked men, they'll die."

Analysis

  • Shows Roland's pride — fears losing his reputation more than death.
  • Durendal — Roland's sword; represents his honor and prowess.
  • "Pagan traitors" — reflects Christian bias against Muslims.
  • This decision leads to the destruction of the rear guard.

Roland's Refusal Lai 87

"Don't speak such foolishness — shame on the heart gone coward in the chest. We'll hold our ground, we'll stand firm — we're the ones! We'll fight with spears, we'll fight them hand to hand!"

Analysis

  • Roland accuses Oliver of cowardice — ironic, since Oliver is the wise one.
  • Shows Roland's reckless bravery.
  • "Shame on the heart gone coward" — reflects chivalric values.

Ganelon's Punishment Lai 289

"Ganelon must die, and in amazing pain. Four war horses are led out and brought forward; then they attach his two feet, his two hands. These battle horses are swift and spirited, four sergeants come and drive them on ahead toward a river in the midst of a field. Ganelon is brought to terrible perdition, all his mighty sinews are pulled to pieces, and the limbs of his body burst apart; on the green grass flows that bright and famous blood. Ganelon died a traitor's and recreant's death."

Analysis

  • Shows medieval justice — severe punishment for treachery.
  • Public execution — deterrence and shame.
  • "Traitor's and recreant's death" — reinforces the gravity of betrayal.
  • Contrast with Roland's death: Roland dies as a martyr; Ganelon dies as a traitor.

Oliver's Warning Lai 82

"I saw the Saracens, no man on earth saw more of them — one hundred thousand, with their shields, up in front, helmets laced on, hauberks blazing on them, the shafts straight up, the iron heads like flames — you'll get a battle, nothing like it before. My lords, my French, may God give you the strength. Hold your ground now! Let them not defeat us!"

Analysis

  • Foreshadowing — Oliver knows they are outnumbered.
  • "One hundred thousand" — exaggeration typical of epic.
  • Oliver's wisdom contrasts with Roland's pride.

7 · Themes & Medieval Values

Major Themes
ThemeExplanationEvidence
Loyalty vs. Betrayal Roland is loyal to king and God; Ganelon betrays them. Ganelon tells Muslims how to ambush; Roland fights to the death.
Pride / Hubris Roland's refusal to blow the horn causes destruction. "I'd be a fool to do it. I would lose my good name."
Chivalry Bravery, honor, service to lord. Roland accepts rear-guard duty; fights bravely.
Religious Devotion / Crusades Christians vs. Muslims; dying in battle earns salvation. Turpin's sermon; angels take Roland's soul.
Feudalism Lord-vassal relationship; loyalty and service. Roland serves Charlemagne; Ganelon violates his oath.
Good vs. Evil Christians portrayed as heroic; Muslims as pagans/traitors. "Pagan traitors came to these passes doomed."
Justice Treachery is severely punished. Ganelon torn limb from limb.
Oral Tradition Assonance, unequal stanzas, jongleur performance. Structure of the epic.

Medieval Values in the Epic

Feudalism

Roland is Charlemagne's vassal; owes military service. Ganelon's betrayal violates the feudal oath.

Chivalry

Roland's bravery, Oliver's wisdom, Turpin's devotion.

Religious Devotion

Turpin promises Paradise; Roland's soul taken to heaven.

Crusading Spirit

Muslims portrayed as enemies of Christ; war is holy.

Patriarchy

Male-dominated society; women are absent or background figures.

Honor / Reputation

Roland fears losing his "good name" more than death.

Justice

Trial by combat; Ganelon's execution.

Martyrdom

Dying in battle for God and king earns eternal salvation.

8 · Practice Q&A Tap to Reveal

A · The Medieval Period

What is the Medieval Period also known as?
The Middle Ages or Dark Ages.
What event marked the beginning of the Medieval Period?
The fall of the Roman Empire.
What institution became the unifying force in fragmented Europe?
The Roman Catholic Church.
What were the Crusades?
Holy wars between Christians and Muslims, started in 1095 AD by Pope Urban II. He promised eternal salvation and pardon of sins to soldiers who would reclaim the Holy Lands.
What is feudalism?
A political-economic system based on land ownership and alliances. Lords grant fiefs to vassals in exchange for protection and services.
What is the manor system?
An economic system where the lord provides peasants with protection, house, and farmland in exchange for their labor, a portion of their earnings, and other services.
What is chivalry?
A code of principles followed by knights — bravery, honor, loyalty, and service.
What role did the Catholic Church play during the Medieval Period?
It was the unifying institution; sent missionaries, seized territories, fought against Islam, and launched the Crusades.
How did the concept of "nation" emerge?
Through territorial claims, cultural similarity, and unity among Germanic tribes. This gave rise to modern European nations like France, Germany, and Spain.
What are the three main social classes in feudalism?
Nobility (lords/vassals), knights, and peasants.

B · The Song of Roland

What is a chanson de geste?
A "song of deeds" — an epic poem about Charlemagne's reign, performed orally by a jongleur.
What historical event is The Song of Roland based on?
The Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778 AD.
How does the epic differ from history?
Historically, Christian Basques attacked the rear guard; the epic changes them to Saracens/Muslims. Eggihard and Anselm are replaced by Oliver and Turpin.
Who is the author of The Song of Roland?
Unknown. The name Turold is mentioned at the end, but his connection is unclear — "declinet" could mean translate, compose, or recite.
Where is the oldest existing manuscript kept?
Oxford University. Old French, dated 1129–1165 AD.
What is the structure of the epic?
Unequal stanzas, assonance instead of rhyme, performed by a jongleur.
Why does Ganelon betray Roland?
Roland volunteers him as messenger to Marsilion, putting his life in danger. Ganelon seeks revenge.
Why does Roland refuse to blow the olifant?
Out of pride — he fears losing his good name and believes they can win.
What is Roland's flaw?
Pride / hubris.
How does Turpin motivate the French?
He reminds them of duty to king and God and promises absolution and Paradise if they die.
How is Ganelon punished?
He is torn limb from limb by four horses.
What is the significance of Roland's death?
He dies as a martyr/hero; his soul is taken to Paradise; his death inspires Charlemagne's revenge.
How does the epic portray Muslims?
As pagans and treacherous enemies of Christ — reflects Crusade propaganda.
What is assonance?
Repetition of vowel sounds; used instead of rhyme in The Song of Roland.
Who is Oliver? How does he contrast with Roland?
Oliver is Roland's friend and the wise, cautious one. He urges Roland to blow the horn. He contrasts with Roland's pride and recklessness.
What is the significance of the olifant?
It is Roland's horn, used to call for help. His refusal to blow it represents his pride; his eventual blowing of it is too late.
What is Durendal?
Roland's sword; represents his honor and prowess. He tries to break it on a rock so it won't fall into pagan hands.
How does the epic end?
Charlemagne defeats the Muslims, captures Saragossa, and executes Ganelon. Roland's soul is taken to Paradise.
What is the role of Archbishop Turpin?
He encourages the French, promises them Paradise, and fights alongside them. He represents the Church's role in the Crusades.
What are the main themes of The Song of Roland?
Loyalty vs. betrayal, pride/hubris, chivalry, religious devotion, feudalism, good vs. evil, and justice.

10 · Oral Recit Tips

How to Answer — Use P-E-S

P — Point

State your answer clearly.

E — Evidence

Give a quote or detail from the text.

S — Significance

Explain why it matters — connect to the Medieval Period or a theme.
Example:
Q: Why does Roland refuse to blow the olifant?
P: Roland refuses out of pride.
E: He says, "I'd be a fool to do it. I would lose my good name all through sweet France."
S: This shows his hubris — he values his reputation more than the safety of his men. This flaw leads to the destruction of the rear guard, making him a tragic hero.

Key Connections to Make

  • Connect Roland's pride to the tragic hero concept.
  • Connect Ganelon's betrayal to feudal disloyalty and medieval justice.
  • Connect Turpin's sermon to the Crusading spirit and religious devotion.
  • Connect the epic's portrayal of Muslims to Crusade propaganda and medieval bias.
  • Connect Roland's death to martyrdom and salvation.

What to Do If You Get Stuck

  • Summarize the plot briefly.
  • Define a key term (feudalism, chanson de geste, etc.).
  • Compare two characters (Roland vs. Oliver; Roland vs. Ganelon).
  • Connect to a theme (loyalty, pride, chivalry).
  • Use a quote you remember.
